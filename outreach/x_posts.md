## Post 1: El Niño (attach `g1_2026_vs_1997.png`; reply with `a1_rain_race.gif`)

```
Uganda was told to expect heavy El Niño rains.

Instead: June–Aug was the driest on record, and Sept is at 54% of normal.

Forecast wrong? Not yet. In 1997, the great El Niño flood year, Uganda was just as dry on 5 Oct. Then the rains came.

The Indian Ocean will decide.
```

**Alt text:** Line chart of Uganda's average rainfall since 1 August. The 2026 line sits at the bottom of the normal range, almost exactly on the line for 1997, the El Niño flood year, which then rose sharply in November.

**Optional reply in a thread** (attach `g2_elnino_iod.png`):
```
Why the uncertainty? El Niño alone doesn't make Uganda's Oct–Dec rains. The Indian Ocean Dipole does. In 1997 the eastern Indian Ocean was cold. In 2026 it's warm. Every strong El Niño since 1981 still gave above-normal rain, but 2026 is stronger than any of them.
```

## Post 2: Kampala floods (attach `g6_kampala_rain_vs_paving.png`; reply with `a2_kampala_growth.gif`)

```
Kampala's floods keep getting worse. Is it climate change?

The city's 10-year storm hasn't grown since the 1980s (84 → 74 mm).

Its paved surface has (57% → 72%).

Paving alone has raised flood peaks ~60% since 1975: 3–5x what climate change adds by 2050.
```

**Alt text:** Two comparisons for Kampala. Ten-year one-day storm: 84 mm in 1981–2003, 74 mm in 2004–2025. Paved and built surface: 57% in 1992, 72% in 2014.

**Optional reply in a thread** (attach `g5_concrete_vs_climate.png`):
```
Same story in Nairobi, Dar es Salaam, Lagos and Johannesburg: the flood risk already built in through paving since 1975 exceeds what mid-century climate change is projected to add. Protect valley floors and hold runoff on site.
```
