# Where is the El Niño rain? What 45 years of data say about Uganda's forecast, its shifting seasons and its floods

*Subtitle: The heavy rains we were promised have not arrived — yet. The answer lies in the Indian Ocean, and in some changes to our seasons that rain gauges alone can't see.*

---

In late July, Uganda was told to prepare for heavy rain. A Ministry of Water and Environment advisory warned of "a high probability of above-normal rainfall episodes in several parts of the country" for September to December 2026, driven by a developing El Niño. In August, the regional climate forum for the Greater Horn of Africa (ICPAC's GHACOF-74) issued a wetter-than-normal outlook for October–December and named 1997 and 2023 as analogue years. Those were two of the wettest short-rains seasons in living memory.

The El Niño did arrive. By September, the sea surface in the central Pacific was almost 3 °C warmer than normal. That makes 2026 one of the strongest El Niños on record, and NOAA now gives it a better than 90% chance of being "very strong" through the end of the year.

The rain did not arrive. I analysed 45 years of satellite and gauge rainfall data for every part of Uganda. Here is what I found.

## 1. 2026 has been unusually dry, not wet

June to August 2026 brought only about 60% of normal rainfall across Uganda. That makes it the driest June–August in the 45-year record for most of the country. From 1 September to 5 October, the country received 54% of normal, a level reached in only 1 year in 50. Karamoja got 19%.

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 1 — `g1_2026_vs_1997.png`]**
> *Caption: Rain accumulated across Uganda since 1 August. 2026 (black) sits at the bottom of the normal range — exactly where 1997 (red), the great El Niño flood year, was on the same date.*

So was the forecast wrong? **Not yet.** Look at the red line. In 1997, the year of the El Niño floods, Uganda was just as dry on 5 October: 53% of normal, almost identical to 2026. Then the rains came in late October and November, and the season ended 37% wetter than normal.

> 🗺️ **[OPTIONAL MAP — `extra/fig14_2026_status_maps.jpg`]**

The four strongest El Niños since 1981 (1982, 1997, 2015 and 2023) all gave Uganda an above-normal October–December. The record is on the forecasters' side. But it is a short record, and 2026 is stronger than any year in it.

## 2. El Niño doesn't bring the rain on its own. The Indian Ocean decides

For Uganda's second rains, the more important ocean is the Indian Ocean, not the Pacific. When the western Indian Ocean (off Somalia and Kenya) is warmer than the eastern side (off Sumatra), moist air is pushed onto East Africa. Scientists call this a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD). In the data, the IOD explains Uganda's October–December rain better than El Niño does, and once you know the IOD, El Niño adds very little.

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 2 — `g2_elnino_iod.png`]**
> *Caption: Each dot is one year. The wet years are strong El Niños with a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (blue). Weak El Niños without it — 1987, 1991, 2009 — were dry.*

The 2026 forecasts assumed the Indian Ocean would follow the 1997 script and turn strongly positive. So far it hasn't. The dipole index is only about +0.5 °C, and the eastern Indian Ocean is *warm* (+0.4 °C), where in 1997 it was cold (−0.8 °C). That cold eastern pole is what powered the 1997 deluge.

**My reading:** history still favours a wetter-than-normal end to 2026, but with much less certainty than the forecasts implied. The forecast was reasonable but over-confident. It should have said that much depends on the Indian Ocean, and that the start of the season could be dry.

### How Uganda could forecast better

- **Make the Indian Ocean the headline predictor** for September–December, not just El Niño.
- **Give probabilities, not adjectives.** "65% chance of above-normal rain" is more honest, and more useful to a farmer, than "heavy rains".
- **Separate the start of the season from its total.** El Niño years often start late and dry.
- **Update every month** as the season unfolds, using the latest ocean data and the rain that has actually fallen.
- **Open the station archive.** Uganda's public daily rainfall records stop in 1986. Every forecast, design storm and drought assessment would improve if the Uganda National Meteorological Authority's archive were digitised and shared.

## 3. Our seasons *are* changing, but not the way most people think

Farmers often say the seasons "aren't what they used to be". The data agree, with some surprises.

> 🗺️ **[OPTIONAL MAP — `extra/fig33_satellite_overview.jpg`]**
> *Caption: Uganda's six rainfall regions on satellite imagery (left) and average yearly rainfall across East Africa (right).*

**The second rains are getting wetter and longer.** September–November rainfall has risen by about 14% per decade in Karamoja and 6% per decade in Acholi. Across the north and Karamoja, the single long rainy season now ends about 5–10 days later each decade. October 2011–2025 was, on average, about 18% wetter than October in earlier decades.

> 📊 **[OPTIONAL GRAPHIC — `extra/fig05_annual_cycle_epochs.jpg`]**
> *Caption: Average monthly rain in each region for 1981–95, 1996–2010 and 2011–25. Note the much wetter October in the latest period.*

**The first rains start a little earlier**, about a week earlier than in the 1980s, most clearly in the south-west.

**But the first season is weakening in a way rain gauges miss.** Satellite measurements of how green and healthy crops are tell a striking story. Outside the Lake Victoria crescent, the share of districts whose crops were in vegetation drought during the first season (March–July) doubled, from 11% in 1982–2003 to 23% in 2013–2025. In the second season (September–December) it fell from 21% to just 3%.

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 3 — `g3_season_swap.png`]**
> *Caption: The first season is weakening; the second is improving. Share of districts with crops in vegetation drought, measured from space.*

> 📊 **[OPTIONAL GRAPHIC — `extra/fp03_vci_seasons.png`]**
> *Caption: The same story year by year since 1982: crop condition in the first season (orange) drifting down, the second season (blue) drifting up.*

Rainfall explains the second-season improvement: there is more rain on more days. It does *not* explain the first-season decline. The amount of rain, dry spells within the season and heat have barely changed in March–July, yet crops are less healthy, especially at the peak of the season in late April and May. The cause may be land use and soils (cleared trees, shorter fallows, tired soils), or rain features that satellites miss. Either way, the first season is less dependable than it was, and rainfall data alone would not tell you so.

**What this means on the farm:** in much of the north, Teso, Lango and the bimodal south, the September–December season is now at least as reliable as March–May. Extension advice should stop treating March–May as the default "main" season. For the first season, the priorities are drought-tolerant, short-cycle varieties, soil and water conservation, and supplementary irrigation.

## 4. It is getting hotter, especially at night

Every temperature dataset agrees that Uganda's nights have warmed, by 0.2–0.34 °C per decade. Unusually warm nights have nearly tripled, from about 22 a year in 1981–1995 to about 60 a year in 2011–2025. Daytime warming is real too, but how much it has risen is less certain, because Uganda's temperature station record is thin.

## 5. And Kampala's floods? Blame the concrete before the climate

Every flood in Kampala is now blamed on climate change. The data point elsewhere. The heaviest one-day storm Kampala can expect once a decade has not grown since the 1980s: about 84 mm then and 74 mm now. What has grown is the paved and built-up share of the city's drainage catchments, from 57% in 1992 to 72% in 2014.

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 5 — `g6_kampala_rain_vs_paving.png`]**
> *Caption: Kampala's storms haven't grown. Its concrete has.*

Rooftops, roads and compounds shed water instantly, and building on valley-floor wetlands removes the city's natural sponges. On their own, these changes have raised Kampala's 10-year flood peaks by about 60% since 1975. That is three to five times more than the extra rain that climate change is projected to add by the 2050s. The same holds, to varying degrees, in Nairobi, Dar es Salaam, Lagos and Johannesburg.

> 🎞️ **[ANIMATION — `a2_kampala_growth.gif`]**
> *Caption: Greater Kampala's built-up surface, 1975 to 2030.*

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 6 — `g5_concrete_vs_climate.png`]**
> *Caption: Across five African cities, the flood risk already built in through paving exceeds what mid-century climate change will add.*

The pattern isn't unique to Kampala. Nairobi and Lagos look the same: flat storms and rising concrete. Dar es Salaam and Johannesburg differ, because their heavy storms really have grown, so they face both problems at once. Even there, the paving already done outweighs the climate change expected by the 2050s.

Climate change will make storms heavier. But the fastest way to fewer floods in Kampala is to stop building on the remaining valley floors, require new buildings to hold back their runoff, and keep drains clear.

## 6. What this means for NDP IV: farms, water, food and infrastructure

Uganda's Fourth National Development Plan (NDP IV, 2025/26–2029/30) already names the problem. It calls rain-fed farming "increasingly unsustainable", blames erratic rainfall for food insecurity, and admits that Kampala's drainage upgrades are "not adequate to address the rainwater runoff". The data above turn those concerns into numbers planners and engineers can use.

### Farming: plant by the data, not by habit

Under the Agro-industrialisation Programme, extension services, seed systems and input supply still revolve around March–May as the main season. The satellite record says the first season has become the riskier one in most of the country. Practical shifts:
- move more planting, inputs and credit to the September–December season where it is now more reliable;
- use short-cycle, drought-tolerant varieties and soil and water conservation for March–July;
- target supplementary irrigation at the first season.

### Irrigation: size it region by region

The Ministry of Water and Environment plans to double the irrigated share of irrigable land, from 0.77% to 1.54%, and to raise water-for-production storage from 52.6 to 280.4 million m³ by 2029/30. A single national design figure would get it wrong. At the peak month, a hectare needs about 0.6 litres per second in Ankole–Kigezi but 1.3 in Karamoja (designed for rain that is reached 4 years in 5).

### Water scarcity: start where the map is darkest

Comparing rain with evaporation, Karamoja is semi-arid, with up to 8 dry months a year. A dry belt across Acholi, Lango and Teso, plus the Ankole rangelands and the Rwenzori rain shadow, has 4–5. Climate models give slightly more rain by mid-century, but also more evaporation. Together with population growth, that means water per person will fall in exactly these places. New water-for-production storage should start there. Dams and water supply sources should be sized for a repeat of the 2008–2010 drought, when the driest 12 months brought only 65–74% of normal rain.

> 🗺️ **[GRAPHIC 8 — `g9_water_scarce_map.png`]**
> *Caption: Where Uganda is short of water: rain compared with evaporation, by county.*

### Food security: rain is only part of the story

The dry June–September of 2026 has already hurt second-season planting. If the October–December rains disappoint, food insecurity in Karamoja and the north will rise into 2027. But Karamoja shows that rain is not the whole story: areas in IPC food crisis rose from none in 2011–2013 to about a third in 2021–2024, and the rise began in the good seasons of 2019–2020. Early warning needs vegetation, market, livestock and security information alongside rainfall. Water tanks and seed alone won't fix it.

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 9 — `g10_karamoja_hunger_vs_rain.png`]**
> *Caption: Karamoja's food crisis against the quality of its rainy seasons, 2011–2024.*

### Roads, drains and cities: design for the city as it will be built

For the Integrated Transport Infrastructure and Sustainable Urbanisation and Housing programmes, including the Greater Kampala Metropolitan Area Urban Development Programme:
- use gauge-calibrated design storms (Kampala's 10-year 1-day storm is about 79 mm);
- add 9–13% for climate change by the 2050s;
- treat land-use control as adaptation: protect the remaining valley floors, and require new developments to hold back their runoff (26–116 m³ per hectare would cancel Kampala's extra runoff since 1992).

> 📊 **[GRAPHIC 10 — `g11_design_numbers.png`]**
> *Caption: Four numbers for NDP IV designers.*

### The cheapest fix: open the data

Every road, drain, dam and irrigation scheme in NDP IV is designed on rainfall statistics. Uganda's public daily rain records end in 1986. Digitising and opening the national weather archive, and issuing seasonal forecasts as probabilities, would improve every design in the plan at very little cost.

## The bottom line

- **Was the El Niño forecast wrong?** It's too early to say. September's dryness is not unusual for an El Niño year. The Indian Ocean will decide whether the heavy rains come in late October and November.
- **Are the seasons changing?** Yes. The second rains are wetter and longer, and the first season is becoming less reliable for crops in ways rainfall data alone don't show.
- **Is it hotter?** Yes, above all at night.
- **Are floods a climate problem?** In Kampala, so far, mostly a concrete problem.
- **What should NDP IV do?** Plant by the data, size irrigation and water storage region by region starting with the driest areas, design drains for the city as it will be built, and open the rainfall archive.
