Kampala flooded again this weekend. Lubigi, Kinawataka, Banda, Kimbejja.

Is it climate change, or is it us? I spent the past weeks testing that with open data: 45 years of satellite rainfall, every mapped drain and culvert in Greater Kampala, half a million building footprints and 50 years of built-up and population maps.

What I found:

🔹 **The storms have not got heavier.** Kampala's wettest day of the year shows no upward trend since 1981. Several recent floods came on ordinary rain: 6 March and 17 September 2026 ranked around the 20th percentile of wet days.

🔹 **The channels have changed.** The stretches near reported flood hotspots stand out for homes on the floodplain, buildings inside the 15 m channel reserve, culverts and bridges, and wetland that is all but gone. A transparent score built from these pressures picks out the hotspot channels (AUC 0.73).

🔹 **The next hotspots can be seen coming.** A score built only from 1975–2000 change predicted where valley floors were built over in 2000–2020 (AUC 0.79). Where it was highest, 4× more valley floor was built over. Today it points to the northern and eastern fringe.

🔹 **205,000 people** now live on low ground within 150 m of a drainage channel, up from 36,000 in 1975.

For NDP IV, KCCA and NEMA, the levers are planning and maintenance: enforce the drainage reserve on the worst stretches, desilt culverts before each season, protect the last wetlands as flood storage, and apply development control on the emerging-risk fringe now.

I have also built an open maintenance map that ranks all 6,914 channel stretches and lets crews and residents log blockages, dumping and flooding in one shared record. Happy to share it with anyone working on Kampala's drainage.
