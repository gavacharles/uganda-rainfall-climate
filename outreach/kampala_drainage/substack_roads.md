# Kampala's roads are drowning in silt

*Gava Charles*

**Subtitle:** I modelled every road in Kampala in every kind of storm. Most of the flooding that closes the city's roads is down to clogged channels, not heavier rain.

**Cover image:** `cover_roads_split_1456x1048.png` (same storm, clean vs silted; recommended) or `cover_roads_1456x1048.png`. Social previews (1200 × 627): `cover_roads_split_social_1200x627.png`, `cover_roads_social_1200x627.png`.

---

On Sunday 11 October, the Kampala–Hoima road at Lubigi went under water before dawn. In March it was Jinja Road at Kyambogo. Before that, Bombo Road at Bwaise, the Northern Bypass, Mukwano Road, Entebbe Road. Every rainy season the same roads close, the same boda riders charge to carry people across, and the same argument follows: the rain is getting heavier.

I wanted to know how much of that is true. So I mapped every road in the city and asked a simple question of each one: when it rains, does this road go under, and why?

## Every road in Kampala

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd45_roads_all.png`. Every drivable road in Kampala, 6,725 km, over the city's hills and valleys.]**

Kampala has about 6,725 km of drivable road, from the Northern Bypass to the smallest lane. They run along the ridges and then drop into the valleys again and again: **794 times a road crosses a drainage channel**, and 636 of those crossings have no bridge, just a culvert under the road.

I cut every road into 100-metre pieces, about 88,000 of them, and for each one worked out how high it sits above the nearest channel and how deep the water gets when that channel overflows, in storms from the ordinary (once every 2 years) to the rare (once a century).

Then I checked the model against the real world. All eight road floods I could find in the news, from Entebbe Road in 2007 to Hoima Road this Sunday, **flood in the model at the places where they were reported** when the channels are silted, and six of the eight even with clean channels.

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd46_roads_flood_10yr.png`. Roads that go under in a 10-year storm, with clean and with silted channels. The numbered circles are road floods reported in the news.]**

## Same storm, different drains

Here is the result that surprised me most. Take an ordinary storm, the kind Kampala gets every couple of years, and run it twice: once with the channels clean, once with them silted up.

> **[GRAPHIC: `x12_same_storm_different_drains.png`. An ordinary 2-year storm: 4 km of road under water with clean channels, 199 km with silted ones.]**

- **Channels clean:** about 4 km of road under water, and about 1,500 people cut off from the city centre.
- **Channels silted:** about **199 km** of road under water, and about **149,000 people** cut off.

Same rain. About 45 times more road under water. The difference is silt, sand, plastic and weeds in the channels and culverts.

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd48_roads_satellite_closeups.png`. Satellite close-ups at Lubigi, Bwaise, Kyambogo–Banda and Entebbe Road: roads under water in a 10-year storm when the channels are silted.]**

## So what actually puts the roads under water?

Since 1990, four things have changed:
1. **Silt** has filled the channels and culverts.
2. **Paving** has spread uphill: roofs and roads now send rain straight into the drains.
3. **Building on the floodplain** has narrowed the space water used to spread into.
4. **Climate change** will make storms heavier in the coming decades.

I ran the model with each of these switched on and off, in every combination, and used a standard method from economics (Shapley values) to share out the blame fairly. Then, because every model rests on assumptions, I ran the whole thing **400 more times**, each time changing 12 of those assumptions within reasonable ranges.

> **[GRAPHIC: `x13_why_roads_go_under.png`. Why Kampala's roads go under: share of the rise in people cut off since 1990.]**

- **Silt in the channels: about 80%** of the rise in people cut off by flooded roads (90% range 64–90%).
- **Paving upstream: about 12%.**
- **Building on the floodplain: about 8%**, and probably more than the model can see, because it cannot measure fill and walls.
- **Climate change: about 9%** extra by the 2050s.

Silt came out as the biggest cause in 399 of the 400 runs.

There is another clue. KCCA's own list of flood-prone roads, together with press reports, fits the version of the model with **silted** channels much better than the clean version: reported trouble spots sit a median of about 300 metres from where the silted model floods roads, against about a kilometre for random spots on the road network. The channels look silted because the floods behave as if they are. (I also tried to see silt from space with 10-metre satellite images. It cannot be done: most of Kampala's channels are narrower than one pixel.)

## Climate change matters more once the drains are clean

There is a twist. If the channels stay silted, climate change barely shows: the roads already flood in ordinary storms, so heavier ones add little. But if the channels are kept clean, climate change **nearly doubles** road flooding by the 2050s.

In other words, maintenance is not an alternative to climate adaptation. It is what makes climate adaptation visible. A city that spends on climate-proof roads without clearing its drains will not see the benefit.

## We built the roads in the water's way

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd49_roads_lubigi_then_now.png`. Lubigi in 1986 (Landsat), with today's roads drawn on, beside today's satellite image.]**

Two-thirds of the road that floods in a 10-year storm sits on valley floor that was wetland or bush in 1986. At Lubigi, the Northern Bypass and its feeder roads were built straight across what was open swamp. Urban roads on low ground beside channels more than doubled, from about 141 km in 1975 to 315 km in 2020.

## The crossings to clear first

Because the model knows both how often each crossing floods and how many people's fastest route into town runs through it, it can rank the crossings where clearing the culvert does the most good.

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd51_roads_crossings_priority.png`. The crossings to clear first.]**

The top of the list:
1. **Jinja Road over the Nakivubo channel:** closes in a 2-year storm when silted, only in a 50-year storm when clean. About 640,000 people's fastest route into town runs through it.
2. **Kibuye–Natete Road on the Nalukolongo.**
3. **Northern Bypass at Lubigi.**
4. **Katwe Road on the Nakivubo.**
5. **Gayaza Road** and **Bwaise Roundabout / Bombo Road** at Bwaise–Kalerwe.

Bwaise Roundabout and Bombo Road flood every 5 years even with clean channels. Those need bigger culverts, not only a shovel.

## What it costs, and what it would save

Keeping the channels clean would avoid about **US$0.5 million a year** in lost trips and delays, and about **US$17 million a year** in flood damage to buildings. That is roughly **six times** what KCCA is allocated each year to maintain both roads and drains (about UGX 10 billion). These are conservative numbers: they leave out lost trade, damaged goods, illness and lives.

## What this means

1. **Desilt before every rainy season, starting with the ranked crossings.** It is the single most effective thing the city can do for its roads.
2. **Fit trash screens upstream of the worst culverts**, so plastic is caught before it blocks the crossing.
3. **Enlarge the culverts that fail even when clean**, starting at Bwaise.
4. **Stop building roads across wetlands.** Where a road must cross a valley, cross it on a bridge sized for tomorrow's storms.
5. **Pair every climate-resilient road investment with a maintenance budget**, or the silt will cancel it out.

## What the model cannot do

This is a screening model built from open data: satellite terrain, OpenStreetMap roads, satellite rainfall and population maps. It does not know the actual size of each culvert or how blocked it is today, and its depths are estimates. That is why I tested 400 versions of it. The kilometres of flooded road are the least certain numbers. The conclusion that silt is the main cause is the most robust one.

A culvert inventory from KCCA or UNRA would make it much sharper. If you have one, or photos of these crossings in flood, I would love to hear from you, and you can log flooded roads on the open map **Kampala Drain Watch: gavacharles.github.io/kampala-drain-watch**.

The storms will keep coming. Whether the roads close is mostly up to us.
