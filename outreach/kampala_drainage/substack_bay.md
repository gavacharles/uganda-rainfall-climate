# We built over Kampala's kidneys

*Gava Charles*

**Subtitle:** Kampala drinks from the same bay its drains empty into. The swamps that once cleaned the city's water before it reached Lake Victoria are half gone. Forty years of satellite images show the bay turning green and murky where the channels pour in.

**Cover image:** `cover_bay_1456x1048.png` (1456 × 1048).

---

Turn on a tap in Kampala and the water most likely came from Inner Murchison Bay, the arm of Lake Victoria on the city's southern edge. The National Water and Sewerage Corporation draws it at Ggaba, treats it and pipes it back uphill to our homes.

The same bay is where Kampala's drains end. The Nakivubo channel, which carries the run-off from the city centre, Kisenyi and Katwe, empties into it at Luzira. The Kinawataka and Kansanga channels empty into it too. Everything that goes into those channels, from storm water, silt and rubbish to sewage from homes that are not connected to a sewer, ends up in the water we drink from.

For most of the city's history there was a filter in between.

## The free treatment plant

Until the 1990s, the Nakivubo channel did not run straight into the lake. It spread out into the **Nakivubo swamp**, a wide valley of papyrus between the city and Luzira. The Kinawataka and Kansanga channels did the same through their own swamps.

Scientists measured what the Nakivubo swamp did to the city's dirty water as it passed through. It took out about:
- **half of the nitrogen**,
- **40% of the phosphorus**, and
- **91% of the faecal bacteria.**

Economists then worked out what it would cost to build a plant that did the same job: **US$1,500–2,900 per hectare, every year.** The swamp did it for free.

## Half of it is gone

Using Landsat satellite images back to 1986, I measured how much green wetland remains in the valleys that drain into the bay.

> **[ANIMATION: `kd_a12_bay_swamps.gif`, with `x11_bay_swamps_1986_2025.png` as the still. The swamps between Kampala and the bay from space, 1986 to 2025.]**

> **[GRAPHIC: `x8_bay_treatment.png`. Green wetland in the valleys draining to Murchison Bay: 1,200 ha in 1986, 587 ha in 2025.]**

- **1986:** 1,200 hectares.
- **2025:** 587 hectares, **a loss of 51%.**
- The Kinawataka valley lost **78%**. The Nakivubo and Kansanga valleys lost **46%** each.

I checked this a second way, with a machine-learning model that tells papyrus apart from gardens, trees and roofs. It gives the same answer, about half gone. In the Kinawataka valley, built and bare ground went from 3% in 1986 to 75% today.

At the 1990s prices, that lost swamp was doing **US$1–2 million of water treatment every year**. In nutrients, it means about **64 tonnes of nitrogen and 9 tonnes of phosphorus a year** that the Nakivubo swamp no longer holds back. The city has more than doubled since those measurements were taken, so the real figure is higher.

## You can see it from space

Satellites cannot take a water sample, but they can see colour. Muddy water reflects more red light, and water full of algae has a green signature. I took every usable satellite image of the bay since 1984, about 900 days of images, and compared the water where the city's channels enter with the open lake further south, on the same day. Comparing on the same day cancels out haze, season and differences between satellites.

> **[GRAPHIC: `kd42_bay_plumes.png`. a: where the channels meet the bay. b: a dry day, June 2026. c: after 37 mm of rain, November 2019.]**

**1. The bay is green where the city drains into it.**
The algae signal at the channel mouths is about **four times** that of the open lake. The colour fades past the narrows at Ggaba, out into the lake. You can see the city's footprint in the water.

**2. It has got worse as the swamps disappeared.**

> **[ANIMATION: `kd_a14_bay_murkier.gif`, with `x10_bay_murkier.png` as the still. As the swamps went, the bay got murkier.]**

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, the water at the channel mouths was about **14% murkier** than the open lake. Today it is **51% murkier**. The step-by-step rise follows the loss of wetland closely: each 100 hectares of swamp lost goes with about 5% more murkiness at the mouths, even after allowing for rain, season and the type of satellite.

**3. Storms make it worse, but the problem is there every day.**
After heavy rain, plumes of brown water spread across the whole inner bay. Panel c above, from November 2019, shows one.

> **[ANIMATION: `kd_a13_bay_plumes.gif`, with `kd42_bay_plumes.png` as the still. The bay on every clear day in two short-rains seasons, December 2018 to January 2019 and November to December 2020, with the rain of the previous three days.]** But the channel mouths are murky and green even after a week without rain. This is not just a storm problem. The city's waste reaches the lake every day, and less and less of it is filtered on the way.

## Why it matters

- **Water bills.** The dirtier the water at the intake, the more chemicals and energy it takes to make it safe. Operators at Ggaba were already reporting more colour, turbidity and algae in the raw water 25 years ago. The satellite record shows the trend has continued.
- **Health.** Faecal bacteria that the swamp once removed now reach the bay. Fishing communities and people who use lake water directly carry that risk first.
- **The lake.** Nitrogen and phosphorus feed algal blooms, which take oxygen from the water and harm fish.
- **Floods.** The same swamps used to hold flood water (that is my previous post, *Kampala paved its sponge*). Kampala lost one piece of land and two services with it.

## What should be done

1. **Treat the remaining 587 hectares as water infrastructure.** Gazette them, stop further conversion, and put them in KCCA's and NWSC's asset plans next to pipes and treatment works.
2. **Restore what can be restored.** Each 100 hectares of swamp brought back is worth roughly **US$150,000–290,000 a year** in water treatment alone, before counting the flood protection.
3. **Connect more homes to sewers**, so the wetlands polish treated water instead of absorbing raw sewage.
4. **Watch the bay from space.** Everything in this post uses free satellite data. NEMA, NWSC or KCCA could track the bay every week and spot plumes after storms.
5. **Open the data.** NWSC monitors the raw water it draws at Ggaba. Publishing those records would let researchers check the satellite signal and track the bay properly.

## A note on what satellites can and cannot say

Satellite measurements of water quality are indirect: they read colour, not chemistry. The numbers here are relative, comparing the channel mouths with the open lake, and are not laboratory concentrations. The link with wetland loss is strong and consistent, but the city's population and sewage loads also grew over the same years, so the swamps are not the only cause. What the satellites show clearly is that the water where Kampala's channels enter the lake is getting worse relative to the lake, and that the swamps which used to stand in the way are going.

The swamps were the cheapest treatment plant Kampala ever had. We are building over the last of it.
